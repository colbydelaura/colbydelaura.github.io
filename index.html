FALCO — UAS Structural Frame

Cleared for public release

FIG. 01FALCO_Frame_1 — single-piece body, modeled in SolidWorks.

ORGU.S. Navy Dept. of War — UAS Dev. (Volunteer)
MATERIALTPU (over PLA)
TEMP RANGE−40°F to −22°F

FALCO is a drone designed to be 3D‑printed at overseas bases in extreme environments — cold, wind, and rough field conditions, with Mongolia as the target case. My job was the body: a single‑piece frame that fits a 290×290×290mm printer envelope and can be built and repaired by field personnel with no engineering background.

The one‑piece constraint mattered as much as the strength constraint — every joint is a failure point in a shed at −40°F. I used SolidWorks to work out the geometry and space claims for every component that had to mount to it, while teammates ran FEA to compare candidate materials. TPU's elasticity over PLA's stiffness was the deciding factor: PLA gets brittle and cracks in the cold, TPU flexes and survives it.

Design constraints solved
  • Fits inside a 290×290×290mm print volume, no assembly required
  • Single piece — no fasteners or joints to fail in the field
  • Material selected for elasticity at −40°F to −22°F, not just strength
  • Space claimed for every mounted component before printing, not after
02

Cargo Drone — Autonomous Payload Delivery

UCLA ENGR 1 DR
ROLEStructure, electronics, controls
DURATION1 quarter
OUTCOMETarget hit — consistently accurate

The brief was open: build a drone that finds a target and drops a payload on it. My team decided to make it entirely ours — a fully 3D‑printed fuselage instead of a kit frame, our own aftermarket motors (twice the power of what UCLA provided, since we were carrying four golf‑ball payloads instead of nothing), and a lattice‑structured body to keep weight down without giving up strength. I soldered all of the electronics myself, and wrote the Python computer‑vision pipeline — running on a Raspberry Pi camera module — that found an orange target and triggered four servos to release the payload.

Articon USA — Mechanical Engineer, Intern

Jun – Sep 2026
FOCUSMetal AM systems, CNC service, data pipeline
TOOLSSolidWorks, Python, Asiga, imes‑icore

One of our head engineers was building an AI‑powered diagnostic system for our CNC machines — effectively an always‑on check‑engine light, sensing anomalies in real time from current, vibration, pressure, and temperature and reporting them to a shared database. My job was the data behind it: a Python program that logged everything about each milling job — start/finish time, duration, material, tool type and condition, workpiece, operation, and outcome — into a CSV the AI agent could train on.

TARC — Competition Rocket

High School · 2nd, Regional Qualifiers
SCHOOLCapistrano Valley HS
ROLECo‑Team Lead (with Spencer)
TARGET1,000 ft / 90s, egg intact

Co‑led a team of six through the Team America Rocketry Challenge — delegating design tasks, overseeing the full build, and running launch‑day data collection. Spencer and I split the rocket in half and each owned a side; I had the bottom half — motor mount, fins, and fuselage.

The rocket came out heavier than the school‑issued Class C motor could handle well, so I made the case to move up to a Class D motor instead of redesigning around the weight. That meant re‑solving the motor mount: I went through trial and error on the 3D‑printed chamber dimensions until the fit was snug enough to hold under thrust but loose enough to remove — my first real lesson in why tolerance is its own design problem, not an afterthought.
